## Stages in full

### 1 · Opener — the question you always get — 5 min · pairs → lockstep

**Purpose.** Three lessons of this unit have been about *telling*. This one is about
*asking*, and the fastest way to make that switch visible is to start from the
questions the learner is already asked all the time.

**Instruction.** "With your partner. **What are the three questions people always
ask you** when they meet you for the first time? In any language — tell me the
questions, not the answers. Two minutes."

**CCQ.** "Questions or answers?" → *Questions.*

**Then, lockstep, 2 minutes.** Collect five or six on the board **in English**, on
the left. *Where are you from? What do you do? Do you have kids? How long have you
been here?* Leave them up. Stage 4 translates them, and the fact that they came from
the group is what makes stage 4 feel like work rather than a worksheet.

**Correction stance.** None.

---

### 2 · Engage — the questions in your recording — 6 min · solo → pairs

**Purpose.** Make the gap specific. Learners have now produced two monologues (L2's
homework and L3's) and asked almost nothing. This stage is where they notice that.

**Instruction.** "Four minutes, on your own. Think about the recording you made
after Lesson 2 — someone else's week. **Write down the five questions you would
have had to ask that person** to get that information. In Italian. Where you can't,
write the English and box it."

**CCQ.** "Questions to the person, or sentences about them?" → *Questions to them.*

**Then, pairs, 2 minutes.** "Swap lists. Did your partner write a question you
couldn't?"

**Correction stance.** None. Collect nothing; stage 4 fixes all of it.

---

### 3 · Input — *Due profili* — 12 min · solo → pairs `[ITA?]`

**Purpose.** Put the same content in front of learners twice, in two registers, so
that the `tu`/`Lei` difference is something they observe rather than something they
are told. This is also the lesson's only reading, and both profiles are **models for
L5's written task**.

**Instruction.** "Two profiles from a language-exchange site. Same website, same
purpose, very different. Read both — four minutes — then exercise 1. **Part B of
exercise 1 is the interesting one:** it asks you what is different about *how* they
write, not about what they say."

#### Profilo 1 `[ITA?]`

> **Claudia, 29 anni, Firenze**
>
> Ciao! Mi chiamo Claudia e cerco qualcuno per fare conversazione in inglese.
> Lavoro in un'agenzia di viaggi e il mio inglese è… diciamo abbastanza buono, ma
> solo per le email!
>
> Sono socievole e abbastanza disponibile: posso chiamarti la sera o il weekend.
> Vivo con due coinquiline, Sara e Bea: sono simpatiche e molto affidabili, ma un
> po' disordinate.
>
> E tu? Come ti chiami? Dove abiti? Che lavoro fai? A che ora ti svegli di solito —
> sei una persona della mattina o della sera? Quando ci possiamo sentire?
>
> Aspetto un tuo messaggio. Ci sentiamo presto!

#### Profilo 2 `[ITA?]`

> **Dott. Marco Ferri, 54 anni, Bologna**
>
> Buongiorno. Mi chiamo Marco Ferri, sono ingegnere e lavoro in uno studio qui a
> Bologna. Cerco una persona di lingua inglese per uno scambio linguistico.
>
> Sono una persona piuttosto riservata e il mio inglese è scolastico, ma sono
> affidabile e puntuale. Vivo con mia moglie; i nostri figli sono grandi e non
> abitano più con noi.
>
> E Lei? Come si chiama? Di dove è? Che lavoro fa? Quando si trova meglio per
> parlare — la mattina o la sera? Quanto tempo ha a disposizione ogni settimana?
>
> La ringrazio e La saluto cordialmente.

**Recognition only, and say so if asked:** `La ringrazio e La saluto cordialmente`
is a fixed formal sign-off. The `La` in it is an object pronoun the course analyses
at U8. Learn the whole line as a phrase, the way `Ci vediamo!` was learned in L2.

**CCQ.** "Do the two profiles say different things, or the same things in a
different way?" → *Mostly the same things, differently.* · "Which one is talking to
you as `tu`?" → *Claudia's.*

**Correction stance.** Accuracy on exercise 1 part A; part B is observation and
nothing is marked.

### 4 · Recycle 1 — question forms, and keeping it going — 14 min · lockstep → pairs `[ITA?]`

**Purpose.** The first half of the L4 contract: **present-tense question forms**,
recycled within A2. And the half of can-do goal 4 that is not about questions at all
— the three or four words that stop a conversation dying between them.

**Instruction, part 1 (4 min, lockstep).** "Look at the eleven questions in the two
profiles. Three things, and the first one is free."

**(a) Yes/no questions.** "`Sei una persona della mattina?` What's the word order?"
→ *Exactly the same as a statement.* "So what makes it a question?" → *Your voice
going up.* Write on the board:

> `Ti trovi bene qui.` ↘ — statement
> `Ti trovi bene qui?` ↗ — question
> **Italian does not reorder the words. Your voice does the work.**

This is a free gift to an English speaker and most learners have never had it
pointed out.

**(b) Question words.** Put them up as a strip: `Come · Dove · Quando · Che cosa ·
Che lavoro · A che ora · Quanto tempo · Con chi · Perché`. These are recycling, not
new: take them from the class in sixty seconds.

**(c) The register switch.** "Now the thing that matters. Claudia says `Dove
abiti?`. Ferri says `Dove abita?`. **Same question, different person of the verb.**
`tu` → `Lei`, and `Lei` takes the **third-person** form, the same one you use for
*he* and *she*." Write the pair up and leave it:

> `Come **ti** chiam**i**? / Dove abit**i**? / A che ora **ti** svegli**?**` — `tu`
> `Come **si** chiam**a**? / Dove abit**a**? / A che ora **si** svegli**a**?` — `Lei`

"And notice: the reflexive pronoun switches too — `ti` becomes `si`. That is
yesterday's lesson, not a new rule."

**Instruction, part 2 (4 min, solo).** "Exercise 2 — eight questions in `tu`.
Rewrite them all in `Lei`. **One of the eight does not change at all** — find it."

**Instruction, part 3 (6 min, lockstep then pairs).** The keep-going devices.
"Asking a question is half of it. The other half is what you say **after** the
answer, and it is five words long."

Write these five on the board and leave them up for the rest of the lesson:

| Device | Use it for |
| --- | --- |
| `E tu?` / `E Lei?` | Hand the same question straight back |
| `Anche io.` / `Io invece no.` | Say whether you're the same or not |
| `Davvero?` | You're surprised and you want more |
| `Ah sì? E perché?` | You want the reason |
| `Anche tu?` | Check you've understood they're the same as you |

Then drill them, thirty seconds, lockstep repetition. Then: "In pairs. I'll give you
one question. **Ask it, answer it, and then keep going for sixty seconds using
nothing but those five things.** Question: *A che ora ti svegli?* Go."

Run it twice, with a second question (`Vai d'accordo con i tuoi vicini?`).

**CCQ.** "To make a yes/no question in Italian, what do I change?" → *Nothing — the
intonation.* · "`Lei` uses which person of the verb?" → *The third — same as
he/she.* · "After your partner answers, what do you say?" → *One of the five on the
board.*

**Correction stance.** Accuracy on exercise 2 and on the `tu`/`Lei` form. The
sixty-second runs are fluency — collect two things for stage 8.

### 5 · Recycle 2 — adjective agreement — 12 min · pairs → lockstep · `A8` `[ITA?]`

**Purpose.** The second half of the L4 contract: **adjective agreement**, recycled
within A2. The items are taken from what L3 actually produced — this is not a
generic agreement exercise, it is yesterday's error board written down.

**Instruction.** "Exercise 3. Eight sentences about people. Some have **one** thing
wrong with an adjective. **Some are already correct**, and I'm not saying how many.
With your partner: decide, fix the wrong ones, and for each one, say **which word**
told you what the ending had to be. Six minutes."

**CCQ.** "Does every sentence have a mistake?" → *No.* · "What do you have to
say as well as the correction?" → *Which word decided the ending.*

**Then, lockstep, 5 minutes.** Go through all eight. For each wrong item the
question is always the same one — "which word decides?" — because that is the only
transferable part. Point at the three columns from L3 stage 5 if you still have
them; if not, rewrite them in fifteen seconds:

> `-o` → `-o / -a / -i / -e` · `-e` → `-e / -i` · `in gamba` → never changes

**Correction stance.** Accuracy, and metalanguage welcome.

### 6 · Culture note — `tu` and `Lei` — 15 min · solo → pairs → lockstep · `A10` `[ITA?]`

**Purpose.** The culture note, and the one that sets up the whole course: the
register axis runs from here to U7's `Lei` imperatives and U11's formal requests.

**This note is written in English, deliberately.** `A10` allows either, and later
units' notes move into Italian as the level rises. Unit 1's does not, for one
reason: the takeaway has to be **unambiguous**. A learner who half-understands a
150-word Italian text about register will make a confident guess, and a confident
guess about `tu` is exactly the error this note exists to prevent.

**Instruction.** "Read this — four minutes. Then exercise 4: three questions on the
text, and two about you. Then we'll talk about the last one."

#### `Tu`, `Lei`, and who moves first

> English has one word for *you*, so an English speaker arriving in Italy has to
> make a choice they have never made before — and most of them make it by accident.
>
> `Lei` is the default with an adult you have not met: a shop assistant you are
> asking a real question of, a doctor, a landlord, a colleague's boss, anyone
> noticeably older. `tu` is the default with people your own age in an informal
> setting, with anyone you have been introduced to socially, and — this one
> surprises people — in most of the service situations where the exchange is three
> words long. A barista will say `tu` to you and mean nothing by it.
>
> The thing worth knowing is **who moves first**. The switch from `Lei` to `tu` is
> offered, usually by the older or more senior person, and usually out loud:
> `Diamoci del tu`, or `Possiamo darci del tu`. You accept it and switch
> immediately. You do not offer it upward. If nobody offers, you stay on `Lei`, and
> staying on `Lei` for years with a neighbour is completely normal.
>
> Titles do related work. `dottore` is anyone with a degree, not just a doctor.
> `ingegnere`, `avvocato`, `professore` are used the way `Mr` is not: as a sign that
> you are placing someone, correctly, in a world where that matters.
>
> Why an anglophone gets this wrong in a particular way: in English, moving to
> first names is *friendly*, so the instinct is that `tu` is friendly too and `Lei`
> is cold. It is not. `Lei` is **neutral**. Using `tu` too early does not read as
> warm; it reads as not having noticed who you are talking to.

**Then, pairs, 3 minutes.** Exercise 4's two comparison questions.

**Then, lockstep, 4 minutes — and this is the stage, not a wrap-up.** One question:
"Your new neighbour, sixty years old, meets you on the stairs. `tu` or `Lei`?" Let
them disagree — they will. Then give the behavioural takeaway, and write it up:

> **Takeaway.** With someone you have not met who is not obviously your peer,
> **start with `Lei` and let them downgrade it.** Being slightly formal costs you
> nothing. The other error is invisible to you and not to them.

**CCQ.** "Who offers the switch to `tu` — the younger person or the older one?"
→ *The older or more senior one.* · "Is `Lei` cold?" → *No — neutral.*

**Watch the clock here.** This stage invites discussion in English and it is the
one most likely to eat stage 7. Fifteen minutes, and stop mid-sentence if you have
to; the takeaway on the board is what stage 7 needs, and everything after it is a
bonus.

**Correction stance.** Accuracy on the three comprehension questions. The comparison
and the discussion are content only — no correction.

### 7 · Activate — interview carousel — 20 min · mingle / groups of 4 · `A5` `[ITA?]`

**Purpose.** Can-do goal 4, performed and assessed. Three rounds, and the register
changes under the learner's feet — which is the thing the lesson has been building
toward and the thing no amount of exercise 2 will teach.

**The standing rule, for all three rounds.** Write it on the board:

> **You may not move to your next question until you have used one of the five
> things on the board.**

That rule is what turns an interview into a conversation. Without it, learners read
questions off a list and the stage is a worksheet with a partner.

#### Round 1 — `tu`, with a peer (6 min, pairs)

**Instruction.** "Find a partner. You are two people your own age who have just
met. **`tu`.** Six minutes, three each. Ask about their week and the people around
them — you have the question strip and the five keep-going words. Remember the
rule."

#### Round 2 — `Lei`, with `il dott. Ferri` (6 min, pairs)

**Instruction.** "New partner. **One of you is the person from profile 2** — Marco
Ferri, fifty-four, engineer, reserved. The other is doing a language exchange with
him for the first time. **`Lei`**, both of you. Three minutes, then swap roles."

**CCQ.** "Which person of the verb do you need?" → *Third — `si chiama`, `abita`,
`si sveglia`.* · "Does Ferri use `tu` back to you?" → *No — he uses `Lei` too, until
somebody offers.*

#### Round 3 — a card, and your choice (8 min, groups of 4)

**Instruction.** "Groups of four. Take a card each, face down. **Your card tells you
who you are and where you are. You decide `tu` or `Lei` — that's the test.** Talk to
each person in your group for ninety seconds. Then, at the end, two minutes: as a
group, **which card was the hard one?**"

**Role cards** `[ITA?]` — one set per group of four:

> **A.** Sei una studentessa di 22 anni. Siete al bar, dopo un corso di italiano.
>
> **B.** Sei la direttrice della scuola. Sei nel tuo ufficio e la porta è aperta.
>
> **C.** Sei il nuovo vicino di casa, hai 60 anni. Vi incontrate sulle scale.
>
> **D.** Sei un collega nuovo, hai 30 anni. Siete in pausa pranzo.

**The answer to "which card was the hard one" is C**, and the group will get there
on their own. A, B and D are decided by the setting; C is decided by age alone,
which is exactly where the takeaway applies. If nobody raises it, ask: "The
neighbour on the stairs. What did you do?"

**Success criterion for the stage.** Each learner:
1. runs **three minutes** without a stall longer than five seconds;
2. asks at least **five** questions;
3. uses at least **two** of the five keep-going devices;
4. in rounds 2 and 3, uses the register they chose **consistently** — the error that
   matters is not choosing `tu` over `Lei`, it is starting with one and drifting into
   the other.

Read criterion 4 out before round 2. It is the one learners would not guess and the
one an Italian actually notices.

**Correction stance.** Fluency throughout. Say nothing during. Circulate with a pen
and collect six things, at least two of them register drift.

## Exercises and answer keys

### Exercise 1 — *Due profili* `[ITA?]`

**Part A — comprehension.** Answer in English or Italian.

1. What does each of them want?
2. Which one gives you more information about the people they live with?
3. Which one tells you when they are free?
4. Which one would be easier for you to answer? Why?

**Key.** 1 Both want a language exchange / conversation practice in English —
Claudia says `fare conversazione in inglese`, Ferri says `uno scambio linguistico`.
· 2 **Claudia** — two flatmates, named, with three adjectives. Ferri mentions a wife
and grown-up children and no detail. · 3 **Claudia** — `la sera o il weekend`. Ferri
asks *you* how much time you have instead. · 4 Open — accept any answer with a
reason. **4 marks**, item 4 for any reasoned answer.

**Part B — register.** Find **four** differences in *how* the two profiles are
written, not in what they say.

**Key** — any four of these:

| Claudia | Ferri |
| --- | --- |
| `Ciao!` | `Buongiorno.` |
| Calls you `tu` — `Come ti chiami?` | Calls you `Lei` — `Come si chiama?` |
| Exclamation marks, dots, a joke about her email English | No exclamation marks at all |
| First name only | Title + surname — `Dott. Marco Ferri` |
| `Aspetto un tuo messaggio. Ci sentiamo presto!` | `La ringrazio e La saluto cordialmente.` |
| `abbastanza buono`, `un po' disordinate` — casual hedging | `piuttosto riservata`, `scolastico` — formal hedging |

**4 marks.** Learners who find the `tu`/`Lei` difference and nothing else have found
the one that matters; give them the mark and point at the other five.

### Exercise 2 — `tu` → `Lei` `[ITA?]`

Rewrite each question using `Lei`. **One does not change.**

1. Come ti chiami?
2. Dove abiti?
3. A che ora ti svegli?
4. Di che cosa ti occupi?
5. Ti trovi bene qui?
6. Hai fratelli?
7. Vai d'accordo con i tuoi vicini?
8. Ci possiamo sentire domani?

**Key.** 1 Come si chiama? · 2 Dove abita? · 3 A che ora si sveglia? · 4 Di che cosa
si occupa? · 5 Si trova bene qui? · 6 Ha fratelli? · 7 Va d'accordo con i **suoi**
vicini? · 8 **Ci possiamo sentire domani?** — unchanged. **8 marks.**

**The two that teach something:**

- **7** — the verb is not the only thing that moves. `i tuoi vicini` → `i suoi
  vicini`. Learners reliably change `vai` → `va` and leave `tuoi` sitting there.
- **8** — the subject is `noi`, so there is no `tu` in it to change. Nothing happens.
  A learner who "corrected" this one has learned a rule as a reflex rather than as a
  rule, which is worth saying to them kindly.

### Exercise 3 — Adjective agreement: error clinic `[ITA?]`

Some have **one** thing wrong. Some are correct. For each one you fix, say which
word decided the ending.

1. La mia coinquilina è molto socievola.
2. I miei colleghi sono molto disponibile.
3. Le mie vicine sono simpatiche.
4. Mio fratello è testarda.
5. Sono una persona affidabile.
6. Le mie coinquiline sono un po' disordinato.
7. Chiara e Petra sono molto in gambe.
8. Il mio collega è riservata.

**Key.**

| # | Verdict | Correction | Which word decided it, and why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | ✗ | socievol**e** | `socievole` ends in `-e`, so it does **not** change for gender at all. The error is over-applying the `-o/-a` pattern — which is a sign the learner has learned the main rule, not that they have failed. |
| 2 | ✗ | disponibil**i** | `i miei colleghi` — plural. `-e` adjectives do change for **number**, just not for gender. |
| 3 | ✓ | — | Correct. `le mie vicine` → `simpatiche`, feminine plural, and the `h` is there to keep the hard `c`. |
| 4 | ✗ | testard**o** | `Mio fratello` — masculine singular. |
| 5 | ✓ | — | Correct. `persona` is feminine but `affidabile` ends in `-e`, so nothing shows. This item is here so that "it must be wrong, I can't see the agreement" is not a winning strategy. |
| 6 | ✗ | disordinat**e** | `le mie coinquiline` — feminine plural. |
| 7 | ✗ | in **gamba** | Nothing decided it. `in gamba` is a fixed chunk and never changes, for anybody. |
| 8 | ✗ | riservat**o** | `il mio collega` — masculine, **despite the `-a`**. This is the unit's trap noun and it is in here deliberately. |

**8 marks**, and the mark is for the verdict plus the correction. Items 3 and 5
score only if left alone.

### Exercise 4 — Culture note `[ITA?]`

**Part A — on the text.**

1. Who normally offers the switch from `Lei` to `tu`?
2. Does `dottore` mean the person is a doctor?
3. The text says `Lei` is not cold. What is it?

**Key.** 1 The older or more senior person. You accept it; you do not offer it
upward. · 2 No — it is used for anyone with a degree. · 3 **Neutral.** **3 marks.**

**Part B — on you.** No key; these are for discussion and nothing is marked.

4. In your language, is there anything that does the job `tu` and `Lei` do? What do
   you change when you talk to someone you don't know — the words, your voice,
   something else?
5. Think of one real person you speak to in Italian or would like to. `tu` or `Lei`?
   What made you decide?

### Exercise 5 — Claudia in `Lei` (homework, written) `[ITA?]`

Rewrite profile 1 as if Claudia were forty-five and writing to a stranger she wants
to be formal with. ~100 words. Keep the content; change the register.

**Model answer** `[ITA?]`

> **Claudia, 45 anni, Firenze**
>
> Buongiorno. Mi chiamo Claudia e cerco una persona per fare conversazione in
> inglese. Lavoro in un'agenzia di viaggi; il mio inglese è discreto, ma lo uso
> quasi solo per le email.
>
> Sono una persona socievole e disponibile: posso telefonare la sera o nel weekend.
> Vivo con due coinquiline, Sara e Bea: sono simpatiche e molto affidabili.
>
> E Lei? Come si chiama? Dove abita? Che lavoro fa? A che ora si sveglia di solito —
> è una persona della mattina o della sera? Quando ci possiamo sentire?
>
> La ringrazio e La saluto cordialmente.

**What it has to do** — four things, and these are the only four:

1. **Every question in `Lei`** — six of them, third-person verb forms.
2. The **greeting and the sign-off** both changed. `Ciao!` → `Buongiorno.`,
   `Ci sentiamo presto!` → a formal close.
3. **No exclamation marks**, and the joke gone. Register is not only grammar.
4. The **content unchanged** — same job, same two flatmates, same availability. If
   the content changed, the exercise was rewritten rather than re-registered.

*Note what the model did **not** change:* `Ci possiamo sentire` stays exactly as it
was, for the same reason exercise 2 item 8 did. And `un po' disordinate` is dropped
rather than reworded — a mild complaint about your flatmates is a `tu` thing to say,
and knowing what to **leave out** is part of register. A learner who kept it has not
made an error; a learner who dropped it has noticed something good, and it is worth
saying so.

## Teacher notes

Unit-level error profile and pacing:
[`unit-01-teacher-notes.md`](unit-01-teacher-notes.md).

**Predictable errors**

1. **Register drift.** The learner opens in `Lei` and is in `tu` within four
   exchanges — usually at the moment the conversation gets interesting, because the
   `tu` forms are the ones they have practised for three lessons. Expect it in round
   2 and round 3 of stage 7 from almost everyone. **Do:** this is why success
   criterion 4 exists and why you read it out before round 2. In feedback, write up
   one drifting exchange verbatim and ask the group to find the sentence where it
   switched. Do not treat it as a grammar error; treat it as a thing that happened,
   because that is what it is.
2. **`*Dove tu abiti?` and `*Fai tu colazione al bar?`** — English question word
   order imported wholesale, with the pronoun dragged in front of the verb or
   inverted. Expect it in stage 4's sixty-second runs, and more in round 3 when
   attention is on the register. **Do:** point at the two board lines from stage 4(a)
   — `Ti trovi bene qui.` / `Ti trovi bene qui?` — and say the pair aloud. The fact
   that Italian needs *no* reordering is a gift, and learners forget it precisely
   because it is easier than they expect.
3. **`tu` possessives surviving into `Lei`.** `*Va d'accordo con i tuoi vicini?` —
   the verb switched and the possessive did not. Exercise 2 item 7 exists for this
   and it will still happen in stage 7. **Do:** one-word correction — "suoi" — and
   move on. It is a late-acquired detail and an A2+ learner who gets the verb right
   has done the main thing.

**Correction stance per stage.** Stages 3, 4 (parts 1–2), 5, 6 (part A) —
**accuracy**, immediate, metalanguage welcome at 5. Stages 1, 2, 4 (part 3), 6
(part B), 7 — **fluency**, delayed, collected and dealt with at stage 8.

**To shorten (−14 min).** Cut round 1 of stage 7 (−6) and take the culture note down
to nine minutes by setting the text as reading for home and running only the
`signor Rossi` question and the takeaway in class (−6); take two minutes off stage 5.
What you must **not** cut is round 3: it is the only place in the unit where the
learner chooses the register rather than being told it, and it is the whole point of
the culture note. A version of this lesson with round 3 removed does not meet can-do
goal 4.

**To extend (+15 min).** Run a fourth round of the carousel in which learners
interview **you** — `Lei`, no cards, ten minutes — and then two minutes on what they
got wrong about you. It is the most motivating version of the stage and it needs no
preparation, but it only works once the three rounds have been done, so it is an
extension and not a substitute.

**A note on this lesson's place in the course.** From Unit 2 onward, L4 re-works
structures from *earlier units* and its content is dictated by the L4 contract, not
chosen. Unit 1's L4 is the only one that gets to recycle within A2, and that makes
it the easiest L4 in the course to write and the least representative. When this
template is judged for whether it scales, **L4 is the stage to be sceptical about** —
see the known weak spots in
[`unit-01-teacher-notes.md`](unit-01-teacher-notes.md).

## Self-study version — 50 minutes

The mingle is the hardest stage in the unit to do alone, and it has a real
equivalent rather than a substitute. Read stage 7's solo row before you start the
lesson: it needs you to arrange one thing.

| Live stage | Solo equivalent | Min |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Opener (pairs) | Write the three questions you always get asked. **Out loud first**, then written. | 3 |
| 2 Engage (solo → pairs) | Identical — already a solo task. Then one extra: go back to your L2 recording and count how many questions are in it. The answer is usually zero, and that is the lesson's premise. | 4 |
| 3 Input (solo → pairs) | Read both profiles twice: once for exercise 1 part A, once **only** looking for part B's four differences. Two passes with different jobs beats one careful pass. | 8 |
| 4 Question forms (lockstep → pairs) | Work the three parts in order. For part 1, cover each expected answer before you read it. For part 3, record yourself: ask the question, answer it as yourself, then say **four** of the five keep-going devices aloud in sequence. Saying them is what makes them available at speed. | 9 |
| 5 Agreement clinic (pairs) | Solo with the key — and before you look at it, write next to each item **which word decides the ending**. That written answer is what you lose by having no partner to argue with, so write it. | 7 |
| 6 Culture note (solo → pairs → lockstep) | Read the note, do exercise 4 part A, check the key. For part B, write both answers out — two or three sentences each. Then copy the takeaway onto the first page of your notebook: it is true for the next eleven units. | 8 |
| 7 Interview carousel (mingle) | **Three things, in this order.** (a) Take the eight questions from exercise 2 and ask them **out loud**, in `Lei`, pausing after each — then find each answer in profile 2. Ferri's profile is your answer key. (b) Do the same with profile 1 in `tu`. (c) Then the real one: **ask a person you actually know** — in any language — the five questions from stage 2, and write their answers up in Italian as a 60-word paragraph. Then read it aloud. | 9 |
| 8 Delayed feedback (lockstep) | Listen to your stage-4 recording once with one question: **did the register stay the same from start to finish?** Write the sentence where it slipped, if it did. | 2 |
| | **Total** | **50** |

**Why (c) and not "imagine a partner".** `A5`'s solo route is **predict-then-check**
against a real person, because the thing being practised is asking a human being a
question and dealing with the answer you actually get. Where asking someone is
genuinely impossible, (a) and (b) carry the register work and the paragraph in (c)
is written about a person you know from memory instead. What is never acceptable is
rehearsing with an imaginary partner, because the answers an imaginary partner gives
are always the ones you were expecting.

## After the lesson

- **Homework.** Exercise 5 — Claudia in `Lei`, ~100 words, self-checked against its
  four criteria — plus the one written question for yourself. (~20 minutes.)
- **Bring to L5:** the **40-second recording** from L1's homework. L5 stage 1 plays
  it back, and the gap between it and the final recording is the clearest evidence
  of progress the unit produces. A learner who deleted it has lost the most
  motivating three minutes of the unit.
